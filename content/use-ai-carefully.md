# Use AI Carefully For This

Status: draft

These uses are not automatically forbidden, but they require stronger review, stronger privacy protections, and clearer professional accountability.

## What this page helps staff do

This page helps staff recognize when an AI use moves closer to evaluation, recommendation, or protected information. The goal is not to ban thoughtful use, but to slow it down and make the review responsibility explicit.

## Higher-risk uses

- Drafting IEP-related language
- Summarizing student data
- Creating transition assessment summaries
- Making recommendations about services or placements
- Using student-facing chatbots
- Using tools that collect prompts or store data
- Any use involving identifiable student information

## What still requires human review

- Whether the task should be done with AI at all
- Whether the tool is approved for the type of information involved
- Whether the output could influence a placement, service, or expectation unfairly
- Whether stronger documentation, team review, or leadership approval is needed first

## Careful-use example: Drafting IEP goal options

AI can offer goal-language options from a fictional or fully generalized skill profile. The IEP team still determines the real goal, baseline, and criteria.

### What makes this a careful use

Final goals must reflect actual assessment and observation data, student priorities, and team decision-making. AI should not invent a baseline or decide what matters most.

### Copyable prompt

```text
This is a fictional example for educator practice. Do not treat it as a real student record.

A transition-program student wants to work in food service. During a familiar work routine, the student can follow a visual checklist independently. When a task changes or the student is unsure what to do next, the student often waits for an adult rather than asking a supervisor or job coach for clarification.

Create three possible annual IEP goal options focused on self-advocacy and task initiation in a work setting.

For each option:
- Use observable, measurable language.
- Include a condition, student action, and criterion.
- Keep the goal appropriate for workplace learning.
- Do not invent assessment data, services, accommodations, or a disability label.
- Add one short note describing what real baseline data a team would need before finalizing the goal.

These are drafting options only. The IEP team must revise the final goal using actual student data, student preferences, and professional judgment.
```

## Ready to draft?

The AI Drafting Lab helps staff turn a general or de-identified situation into a more deliberate prompt. It does not make a sensitive use automatically appropriate; staff still need to confirm that the tool, information, and purpose are approved and appropriate.

## Information staff should not enter casually

- Protected student records
- Detailed assessment data tied to a named student
- Behavior, disability, or medical information that could identify a student
- Draft recommendations about placements or services framed as if already decided
- Any prompt content that the team would not be comfortable defending later

## Questions to ask before using a district-provided AI tool

Access does not automatically tell staff which data uses the district has approved. Before entering identifiable student information, ask:

- Am I using the district-managed education version with my staff account?
- Has the district approved identifiable student information for this specific purpose?
- What uses are allowed, limited, or prohibited?
- What privacy, retention, access, and data-loss-prevention controls apply?
- Who can answer questions when a use falls into a gray area?

Google states that Gemini used with a Google Workspace for Education account has enterprise-grade protections: chats and uploaded files are not reviewed by humans or used to improve generative AI models. That may support a district-approved use, but it does not replace local policy, a legitimate educational purpose, minimum-necessary information, or human review.

Read: [Google's education data-handling guidance](https://support.google.com/gemini/answer/14620100?co=DASHER._Family%3DEducation&hl=en) and [U.S. Department of Education guidance on online tools and FERPA](https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/faq/i-want-use-online-tool-or-application-part-my-course-however-i-am-worried-it-violation-ferpa).

## A concrete example: initials are not enough on their own

Even in a district-approved tool, replacing a student's name with initials does not automatically make a prompt safe. A disability, a particular job site, a teacher, a school, or an unusual set of needs can still make the student recognizable to people who know the context.

### Still too identifying

> “J.D. is an 18-year-old at Lincoln High with an intellectual disability. He needs extra processing time while learning tasks at the Green Street Cafe. Summarize his work evaluation.”

### Safer starting point

**De-identified notes:** “Completes familiar multi-step tasks independently after a visual model. Asks for clarification when a task changes. Benefits from a brief written checklist when learning a new routine.”

**Prompt:** “Organize these notes into strengths, support needs, and next instructional questions for a draft work-evaluation summary. Do not make recommendations or conclusions.”

Use only the minimum information needed, follow district approval and policy, and review the draft against the original record before using it.

An approved tool may allow specific student information under local rules, but approval is not blanket permission to paste every detail from a record.
